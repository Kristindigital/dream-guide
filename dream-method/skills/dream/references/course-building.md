# Building a course (any platform)

Read this whenever their product is a course, mini-course, workshop, program or membership: when they choose it in Define, when you build it in Execute, and when you set it up in Apply on any platform (Skool, Systeme.io, Stan Store, Payhip, Gumroad, Beacons or anything else). Skool has its own section at the end.
These rules come from Kristin's course-building training, adapted for a beginner. Use them to shape what you suggest. Bring one idea to the student only when it helps the decision in front of them, in one or two plain lines.

---

## 1. First, right-size it

Two very different things are both called "a course":

| | **Mini-course** (most first products) | **Full course or program** |
|---|---|---|
| What it solves | One narrow problem, solved completely | The whole journey, every sub-problem |
| Size | 3 to 5 lessons (blueprint 8 in `execute-blueprints.md`) | Modules, many lessons, resources, often calls and a community |
| Price | Usually $7 to $47 | Higher, and it needs live support to earn it |
| Build time | Fits the 7 days | Expect about 3 times longer than they think. A short preview course takes 1 to 2 weeks; an in-depth course takes months. |

- **For a first product, steer to a mini-course** unless they already have a proven method and students waiting. Say why in one line: "Let's solve one problem completely first. That's what gets you sales and reviews you can build on."
- **A full course can still start now:** pre-sell a smaller first version, or open a founding group, and build the rest around real students' questions. Never promise lessons they haven't made yet as if they're ready.
- **Set a real finish date with them.** Work expands to fill the time it's given. A date, told to someone, gets it done.

## 2. The non-negotiables (honesty and voice)

- **They only teach what they've actually done.** Before you outline, check: "Have you done this yourself, or helped someone do it?" If not, narrow the topic to what they have done, or pick another product. Their credibility depends on it.
- **Their stories, examples and know-how come from them.** You interview, organize and format. You never write a lesson's teaching from general knowledge, and you never invent a story, a result or a student win (`rules.md`). Their training puts it plainly: if AI can teach the course, there's no reason to buy it. Their personality is what nobody can copy.
- **Keep their words verbatim.** When you organize what they said, move it into the best order, mark gaps and redundancy, and suggest visuals. Don't reword it unless they ask. Flag anything above a 4th or 5th grade reading level and offer a simpler version for their OK.
- **If they ask you to "just write the lessons":** explain in one line, then offer the interview instead: "Your buyers are paying for your way of doing it. Talk me through it and I'll organize every word."
- **Product first, profit second.** The goal is to solve a pressing problem with the least time, effort and sacrifice for the buyer. The easier you make it for them, the more the course is worth.

## 3. What a good course does

- **It answers the questions people ask them over and over.** A buyer should be able to get the result from the course alone. Calls, if any, help with their own situation; they aren't required.
- **It covers more than the knowledge gap:** the doubts, the confusion and the shiny distractions that stop people from acting.
- **It's a journey:** from where the buyer starts (usually a pain) to the result, one step at a time, with the questions and roadblocks handled along the way.
- **Show, don't just tell.** Screen shares, live demos, real examples. Nothing left to guessing.
- **Short and focused lessons.** No fluff, but no shorter than they need to be. Brevity is a feature.
- **Both the why and the how.** The best courses teach how to think about it and then show exactly how to do it. Principles alone (like a YouTube talk) or steps alone (like a quick tutorial) are weaker.
- **Fewer, better options.** Too many choices cause decision fatigue, and tired people don't act. Teach the 1 to 3 best ways, matched to who each one is for, not every way that exists.
- **A quick check for an existing course:** Do buyers get the result? Is it easy to use, or do they have to research a lot? Do they finish it? Do they recommend it without being asked?

## 4. Outlining the course (with them, in rounds)

Do this before any slides. Their training calls it the hardest part and the one that makes everything else fall into place. Expect the outline to change several times, and say so: it's normal.

1. **Where the buyer starts.** From their notes: who it's for and what that person already knows. Teach to that starting point, not to where the student is now.
2. **Brain dump their journey.** Ask, a few at a time:
   - "What were the turning points when you learned this? What changed each time?"
   - "What did you get wrong at first?"
   - "What questions do people ask you about this, again and again?"
   They can talk (voice-to-text) or type. Messy is fine.
3. **Add the buyers' problems.** Use the buyer words, reviews and struggles from Research, plus any questions from their DMs, comments, calls or email replies.
4. **Pair each problem with a lesson.** One lesson solves one problem completely.
5. **Put them in order:** first the beliefs and ideas that change how they see it, then the habits, then the step-by-step actions. Fundamentals first, so nothing later is built on a gap. The first quick win comes early.
6. **Group lessons into stages** only where it helps (stage 1 gets the first result, stage 2 builds on it). Too many stages confuse people.
7. **Check the four pillars:**
   - **Sequence:** does each lesson lead to the next, in an order a beginner understands?
   - **No repeats:** every lesson adds something new.
   - **Clear structure:** plain titles that say exactly what each lesson solves, so people can skip what they don't need. Clear beats clever, every time.
   - **Action steps:** clear next steps after the lessons that have something to do.
8. **Cut what doesn't move them toward the result.** Length isn't value. Remove busywork, filler ebooks and anything copied from others that they don't fully understand. Keep what saves the buyer time: templates, checklists, calculators, live help.
9. **Ask the momentum question:** "What does a buyer need to get a small win each week (or each lesson)?" Anything that doesn't help with a win is a candidate to cut.

## 5. What's included (the deliverables)

- **The deliverables are the whole experience,** not just videos: lessons, action sheets and templates, any access to them (comments, posts, messages), any live calls, and the result it's built to get.
- **Match them to the buyer.** Busy buyers value templates and short lessons they can use right away. Buyers with more time may enjoy fuller lessons. Shorter pieces help almost everyone.
- **Specific beats broad.** A lesson that fully solves one problem is worth more than one that touches ten.
- **Right-size support to the price.** A low-price mini-course needs great lessons and action sheets, not calls. A higher-price program usually needs three things: a place to ask questions, predictable live calls and the resources. One-on-one time is the most valuable thing they can offer, so it's never given away cheaply.
- **Let objections shape it.** If buyers keep worrying about one thing, add the piece that removes it (for example a "busy schedule" version, or a fill-in template).

## 6. Action sheets

- **Every action sheet has 4 parts:** a title, the goal (one line on what it helps them do), the material (fill-in questions, prompts or a template) and a **deliverable** (the usable thing they walk away with, like a finished promise statement or a filled-in plan).
- **No deliverable, no action sheet.** Never add sheets to make the course look bigger. Roughly one every 2 or 3 lessons, more in hands-on sections.
- **Format:** a PDF for sheets that won't change. A view-only Google Doc link for sheets they'll update (buyers make their own copy). Put each sheet under the lesson it belongs to.
- **Explain how to use it:** in the lesson video if every buyer needs it, or in a short separate video attached to the sheet if it's optional.
- **Shareable sheets build accountability:** a sheet buyers post in the community or bring to a call gives them something specific to ask feedback on.
- **You can build them:** in their Canva as a fill-in PDF (blueprint 2 style), or as plain text they paste into a Google Doc.

## 7. Writing the lessons and making the slides

**The workflow** (it saves a lot of time):
1. One document per lesson, with the outline points as headings.
2. Under each heading, they talk (voice-to-text) or type everything they know. They mark any gaps to fill later.
3. You reorder it into a clear, step-by-step lesson, keeping their words verbatim. You flag repeats, gaps and spots where a picture would explain it better.
4. You build the slides in their Canva from that exact text (Presentation, 16:9).

**Slide rules:**
- Simple. A light background with dark text, because it's easier to read.
- Bold text 1 to 3 times per slide at most, and not on every slide.
- Each new section gets its own title slide.
- A visual every 4 or 5 slides: a diagram, a screenshot, an example, a real photo. Prefer their own visuals and real screenshots over AI images, so it feels like them.
- Mix the layouts: some slides with just a few words, some fuller "magazine" slides with examples.
- A full lesson video often runs 20 to 60 slides. A mini-course follows blueprint 8 (one idea per slide).
- Slide text should prompt the idea, not script it. Put their talking points in the speaker notes so they can explain and tell stories naturally.
- Comparisons and similes help: link the new idea to something they already know.

## 8. Recording (simple setup, good sound)

Tell them, in a few lines, when they're ready to record:
- **Sound matters most.** People forgive a shaky picture but not bad audio. A basic clip-on or USB mic is worth it. Free audio-cleanup tools can fix light background noise.
- **The rest can be simple:** a phone camera is fine, a window gives good light, and the background just needs to be clean and calm.
- **Tools:** a screen recorder (Loom or similar) and a basic editor (iMovie is free on a Mac).
- **Film each lesson in one go.** After a mistake, pause for about 10 seconds and start the sentence again. The silence is easy to find and cut later.
- **Delivery:**
  - Slow down. A steady, unhurried pace with pauses lets buyers keep up.
  - Let their energy and personality show: smile, keep their face and hands visible, share a story or a quick tangent.
  - Follow the outline but don't read a script word for word.
  - Ask buyers questions, and do the action sheet with them on screen.
  - Rehearse the slides a few times first.

## 9. The "Start here" lesson (every platform)

- It tells buyers what's inside and the order to take it in, how to get help, how to use the course (for example "do the action sheet after each lesson") and the one thing to do first.
- **Record it last,** once the course is done. A quick temporary version is fine if they launch early.
- The first days decide what buyers expect from everything after, so this lesson matters more than it looks.

## 10. Selling it

- **Sell the result, never the hours.** "A step-by-step system to ___" beats "10 hours of video". Nobody buys a long flight; they buy the vacation.
- **Order the list of what's included:** the most valuable item first, the second most valuable last, and the supporting items in the middle (people skim the middle). When speaking (a video or a live call), build up and save the best for last, framed as a bonus.
- **Bonuses only if they're real,** and any limit (like "only for founding members") only if it's true (`why-people-buy.md`, real urgency).
- **Keep updating it.** A course is never finished. They improve it from buyers' questions and what changes in their field, and they say so.

## Skool: building the course inside it

Use this with `skool.md`, Step 4 (the classroom) and Step 5 (community categories).

- **Classroom:** a short "Start here" course (section 9), then their product course. In a bigger program, each big topic is its own module or course, and each lesson solves one problem, with a plain, searchable title.
- **Action sheets:** add each one under its lesson (New Page → Edit → ADD → Add resource file, or paste the Google Doc link), with a few lines under each video saying what's in it, plus the slides or a transcript if they have them.
- **Pre-selling?** Keep each unfinished module as a draft (the course's publish toggle) and publish it on the date they promised.
- **Community categories for accountability:** "Questions & feedback" and "Wins". Invite members to post their finished action sheet for feedback. It keeps them going, and it gives them something specific to ask about.
- **A pinned "Start here" post** that points to the Start here course.
- **Live calls (only if they'll host them):** put them on the Skool calendar at the same times each week, with a topic for most calls and some open Q&A. Ask members to bring their question in 4 parts: where they are now, what's stuck, the result they want, and the question. Save recordings in a "Call recordings" course, labeled by topic.
- **Check in on quiet members** now and then with a friendly post mention or message. Not surveys, and not every week.
- **Share real wins** (with permission) as posts: the before, the doubts, what they did and the result. Members who don't relate to the creator often relate to another member. Real stories only, never made up.
