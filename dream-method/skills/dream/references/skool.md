## What Skool is, and when it fits

Skool is one simple place for a **community** (posts and conversations), a **classroom** (courses, lessons and downloadable files) and a **calendar** (live calls and events). Members join through an About page.

It fits when:
- **Their product is a course, membership or community,** with ongoing support or live calls.
- **They want a free community that grows their audience,** and sells their product inside it. A free group can sell a course (including a PDF or template inside it) for a **one-time "Buy now" price**. That makes Skool a good home even for a single planner or ebook.

It's not the best fit when they just want a shop for downloads with zero community. Etsy, Gumroad or Payhip are simpler for that.

**Why a community makes their product worth more:** information is everywhere now, and AI answers most questions for free. What people pay extra for is confidence that they're doing it right: feedback, live calls, a place to ask. A course plus access to them is worth more than a course alone.

**Skool's search (Discovery)** can bring them members who already use Skool, like walk-in shoppers in a mall. Write the group description and About page in the words their buyer would search, so Skool shows the group to the right people.

## Choose the setup (keep it simple)

Pricing options in Skool: **Free**, **Subscription** (monthly or yearly, optional 7-day free trial), **Freemium** (a free plan plus one paid upgrade), **Tiers** (several paid plans) and **One-time payment** (lifetime access).
Each course in the classroom can also be **Open**, **Level unlock** (members unlock it by taking part), **Buy now** (a one-time price), **Time unlock** (after X days) or **Private**.

Recommend one of these three:

| Setup | Best for | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Free community + product inside** | Beginners growing an audience who sell a planner, ebook or template pack | A free group. A free "Start here" course. Their product as a **Buy now** course ($7 to $47). |
| **Paid membership** | Students who can deliver something every month (new templates, live Q&A) | A subscription group (monthly, plus a yearly option). |
| **One-time course** | A mini-course or program with lifetime access | One-time payment group, or a Buy now course. |

Keep it simple: **one group, at most one upgrade.** A pile of separately paid classrooms confuses buyers, and confused buyers don't buy (or regret it).

**The price changes what members expect.** Most DREAM students start free or low price.
- **Free:** people join to look around. A free starter course is the value, and the About page does the selling.
- **Low price:** members mostly join for the classroom (their product), maybe with a weekly Q&A. The About page sells, with proof and a clear list of what's inside.
- **Higher price** (usually sold on a call first, `sales-calls.md`): the live calls and access to them are the value. They welcome every member personally, and never sell extra classrooms inside it.

## The build, step by step

Walk them through it like every platform: **3 to 5 steps per message,** then wait for "done" or a screenshot. Build every graphic for them in Canva, in their product's look (style sheet). They do the clicks for their account, payouts and identity.

The community itself takes **a day or less** to set up. It's the shell; their product is what takes time. Don't let the setup become the project.

**Ready to open when they have** (drafts are fine, everything can change later):
- who it's for and what it helps them do
- a name (a placeholder is fine) and a simple icon
- the Start Here post, pinned
- a draft About page
- if it includes live calls, at least two on the calendar at launch. If the course isn't finished yet, add more calls to fill the gap.

### Step 1: Name and look (their brand)

A first impression sticks, whether they plan it or not. Their community should look like something their buyer already trusts.
- **Name:** short and memorable. One to three syllables is the sweet spot, and two short words are fine. It should hint at what it does. It can change later, so don't let it stall them. Suggest 5 options from their niche and promise. Avoid long generic AI-sounding names.
- **Colors:** two main colors plus black and white. Use their product's palette. Match the buyer and the price: soft and warm for busy moms, clean and simple for professionals, more polished as the price goes up. A playful look turns away serious buyers, and the other way around. Light backgrounds with dark text read best (`sales-pages.md`).
- **One action color** for buttons and arrows in their graphics, the same every time. Kristin suggests red or yellow, chosen as part of their palette so it doesn't look random.
- **A short slogan:** their promise in a few words (`why-people-buy.md`, section 5), for the thumbnail, graphics and buttons. The full promise goes on the About page.
- **The word they call it** shapes its value: a "program", "playbook", "sprint" or "system" sounds different from "a course". Pick the word that's true to what it is (a single PDF isn't a "system").
- **Icon:** tiny (smaller than a coin), so no words. One simple 2D symbol, at most two colors. If their logo has words, make a simpler version for Skool. Build it in Canva.
- **Profile photo:** their real face, shoulders up, clear and smiling. Not a logo or a far-away photo. It builds trust in every post and message.
- Save their name, colors, fonts, slogan and icon in their notes (their style sheet), so everything matches. If they'd like, build it as a one-page brand sheet in Canva too.

### Step 2: Create the group and choose the price

1. Sign up with Kristin's link and create a community. Follow Skool's setup screens.
2. Choose the pricing model (setup table above). For a Buy now product course, the group can stay free.
3. Turn on a yearly price for memberships if it fits. Many members like it.

### Step 3: Connect payouts

1. **Settings → Payouts → Add payout method** (or the group's **Settings → Pricing → Add payout method**).
2. Choose the country of their bank, confirm the code sent to their email, and enter their name exactly as it is at their bank.
3. Verify their identity (scan the QR code, get a link by SMS, or continue on the same device). Their camera needs to be allowed.
4. Add their local bank account.
5. Decide on **Growth Boost** in **Settings → Discovery** (see the facts above).

### Step 4: The classroom

Their product lives here. If it's a course, build its content with `course-building.md` (outline, lessons, action sheets, slides, Start here, and its Skool section). Make it easy to find everything:
1. **A short "Start here" course:**
   - a quick welcome (a short video or a few lines)
   - how to use the community
   - the one thing to do first
2. **Their product course:** one course, lessons numbered in order, each with a plain, searchable title (no clever movie-style titles). Label lessons so members can skip what they already know. Don't give every video its own course: a few courses with numbered lessons are easier to find your way around.
3. **Files:** to add their PDF or template link, open the course → **New Page** → **Edit** → **ADD** → **Add resource file**. Put the file under the lesson it belongs to.
4. **Under each video:** a few lines saying what's in it (so members can skip what they don't need), the files for that lesson, and the slides or a transcript if they have them.
5. **Course covers:** simple graphics (one to three words, a simple image, or both) in their colors, all in the same style so the classroom looks like one set. You build them in Canva, in their own design, not a copy of another community's.
6. **Access:** set each course in its menu → **Edit course**: Open, Buy now (set the price), Time unlock, Level unlock or Private.
7. **Not finished yet?** (for example, they're pre-selling) Keep unfinished courses hidden as a **draft** with the course's publish toggle, and publish each one when it's ready. Don't lock finished lessons without a reason: members like going at their own pace.
8. **Live calls:** if they host them, add a "Call recordings" course with each recording labeled by topic. If the list gets long, keep the last few months.

### Step 5: Community categories

Only create categories for the behavior they want to see. A simple start:
- **Questions & feedback**
- **Wins** (members share progress)
- **General**
- **Announcements** (only they can post)
- **Social** (optional: only if they want members chatting about life, not just the topic)

They set the example by replying to and liking the kinds of posts they want more of.
In a paid community, they reply to every post within a day. In a free one, they reply as often as they can. Say how fast replies come in the Start Here post, so members post in the group instead of sending messages.

### Step 6: The Start Here post (pin it)

Write it for them, in their voice, and have them pin it as the first post. Shape:
1. A warm, excited welcome to {community name}.
2. Why they started it, in one or two lines of their own story.
3. **Your next steps:**
   1. Start the "Start here" course.
   2. Introduce yourself in the comments (who you are and what you want help with).
   3. {Get the product, or join the next call}.
4. What they'll share inside, so members can {reach the goal}.
5. A warm sign-off.

Its job is to make new members excited and show them exactly what to do first. Optional: a short screen-recording video (Loom or similar) showing where everything is.

### Step 7: The About page (their billboard)

The About page is what people see before they join, so every word must earn its place. Kristin's beach rule: people want the water (the result), so don't crowd the beach with extras.

In a free or low-price community, the About page does the selling, so the steps below matter most. In a higher-price program sold on a call, people are already sold when they see it. There, keep it simple and on brand: their face (ideally doing what they teach), a short description, and a "book a call" or "apply" link.

Strong About pages always have two things: a clear promise and real proof.
1. **One-sentence promise** (their promise sentence, `why-people-buy.md` section 5).
2. **Proof, only if it's real:** their own story or result ("I went from running out before payday to planning every paycheck"), or real member wins shared with permission. No proof yet? Use their story and "what you can expect". Never invent results, numbers or testimonials.
3. **What's inside, in 3 or 4 bullets, each tied to a result.**
   - Good: "Weekly live Q&A where you get your budget checked."
   - Not useful: "a welcoming community", "100 hours of content" (sounds like work), "checklists" (a tool, not a result).
4. **Who it's for, and who it isn't for** (one line each).
5. **An honest reason, if there is one:** why it's free, or why the price is what it is. Urgency only with a true "because" (for example "Founding members pay $9 until we launch the full course on {date}"). Never fake limited spots. **No inflated "value" totals** ("$997 value!") unless they genuinely sell that piece at that price.
   - Scarcity is limited spots; urgency is limited time. Both must be real, and say why: "Because I reply to every post myself, I'm keeping it to 30 founding members."
   - In a free community, the honest reason to join now is what waiting costs them.
6. **A clear call to action that says exactly what to click:** "Click **Join** to get {the result}" (use the exact words on their button). Never "learn more" or "check it out". Confident, never begging (no "please join").

Also:
- **Graphics:** two or three, built in Canva in their look: a promise graphic, a "what's inside" graphic, and a proof graphic only if the proof is real. Fewer is stronger.
- **An optional short intro video** (talking points you write for them, about a minute). Only true things, from their notes:
  - a hook in the first few seconds that says what they'll get (skip "Hi, my name is...")
  - one line of credibility within about 15 seconds: what they did and what they overcame
  - the story in a few sentences, only the parts that matter to their buyer
  - real proof, if any (a member's win shown on screen, with permission)
  - a common worry answered with a real story instead of an argument ("No audience?" becomes a real member who started with none)
  - why they made this: they loved it, people kept asking, or the problem they kept seeing
  - what to click, said at least twice, confidently. Never "please".
  - honest urgency only, with a "because"
  Pair it with the promise and 2 or 3 graphics, because many people won't watch a video. Students who'd rather not film can skip it.
- **Description:** one plain sentence, like an elevator pitch.
- **Links:** two at most, each moving people toward the product (for example their product course and their free guide). A link to something free should lead on to the product.
- **Thumbnail** (it's how they show up in Skool's search): one to three words plus one image (their face, logo or product). Build it in Canva.
- **Look at what works:** study the About pages and thumbnails of growing communities with the same buyer (not necessarily the same product): their words, the worries they answer, their button text. For ideas only, never copied.

### Step 8: Plugins worth turning on

- **Membership questions** (all plans):
  1. A question that shows if they're a fit ("What's your biggest money struggle right now?").
  2. Their email, with a line saying how it will be used (for example "so I can send you the free payday checklist").
  3. Where they found the community.
- **AutoDM** (Pro, for a free or low-price community): a short welcome in one of two shapes. Then reply personally.
  - An easy either-or question, which people actually answer ("Hey #NAME#, welcome! Are you budgeting for your family, or just for you?").
  - Or a direct link to the next step (their product, or a free call).
  - Never an open question like "Why did you join?". It takes thinking to answer, so people skip it.
  - In a higher-price program, skip the automation and welcome each new member personally within a day.
- **Onboarding video** (Pro): a quick tour of where everything is.
- **Links** (Pro): their support email and the rules.

### Step 9: The first 7 days for a new member

Most people decide in their first week whether a community was worth it. Help them get one quick win:
- a welcome (AutoDM, or a personal message)
- the Start Here post and course
- one small action they can finish in a day (for example "fill in your foods list")
- a reply to their introduction

Match it to the price. Low price: a short roadmap (a written plan or a short video), or a group welcome call once or twice a week. Higher price: a one-to-one welcome call that maps out their first week. Either way, tell them which calls, lessons and posts to use first.

### Step 10: Selling inside the community (honestly)

- **The product as a Buy now course:** members see it in the classroom with its price. Announce it with one clear post: the problem, what's inside, the result, and what to click.
- **Posts can be emailed to all members** (an admin option when posting). It's powerful, so use it sparingly, for real news.
- **A short video in the post** (Loom or similar) when they release a new lesson, resource or offer gets more people to look and click.
- **Check in by message** now and then to see how members are doing. In a free community, a check-in is also a natural moment to mention the product or the next live call, if it fits that person.
- **Their own email list:** the membership question that asks for an email (and says how it will be used) is how they collect emails with permission. The **Export** button on the Members tab downloads the answers. Only email people who agreed.
- **Skool has no add-on at checkout.** The next product becomes a second Buy now course, announced with one honest post (`upsells-and-funnels.md`).
- **Offer an upgrade only when it truly helps.** If members ask for more help, telling them about the paid course or membership is helping, not pushing (`why-people-buy.md`, section 12).
  - The best upgrade offer is personal: a message to a member who'd clearly benefit, saying why, then a conversation, not a pitch.
  - In a low-price group, a live workshop inside the community works too: 30 to 40 minutes of real teaching, then the upgrade as the next step for people who want help doing it.
  - Taking what they already paid off the upgrade price is fair and works well. Check that their checkout can do it before promising it.
- **Check it like a member:** use Skool's pricing preview, and view the classroom as a member would. Fix anything confusing.

## Posting inside the community (Market)

When they reach Market, these shapes help them post. Write every post in their voice:
- **Resource post:** "How to {get a specific result}." A line on why it matters, then 3 bullets of what the video or guide covers, then "Check it out."
- **Win post:** only real wins (theirs, or a member's shared with permission).
  - Where they started, and the moment things changed.
  - Three things that made the difference.
  - Why it matters to them.
  - A warm thank-you.
  - No exaggerated numbers.
- **Value post:** "The simple {topic} plan." A relatable starting point, then 3 short steps with real tips, one key insight, and an invitation to the next step (their product or a call). No invented student results.
- **Add a short video** (Loom or similar) to teaching posts. Good posts can be reused on their other social accounts too.
- **Neighboring communities:** their buyer is in other groups too (a gym and a supplement shop share customers). Join a couple that share their buyer but don't compete, and be genuinely helpful there. Follow each group's rules about promotion.
- **Live calls:**
  - About an hour, at a regular time they can keep.
  - Pick an hour their members can make, even if it isn't the easiest for them. Late morning US Eastern time can suit both US coasts and Europe.
  - Members bring questions in this shape: where they are now, where they want to be, what's stopping them, and their question.
  - Take questions in order, and don't let one person take the whole call. More calls a week means shorter calls.
- **Guest experts (later, as it grows):**
  - someone who teaches what they can't
  - at a regular time (the same slot every week or month), not random one-offs
  - Guests usually come for referrals, teaching experience, pay, or a swap (each teaches in the other's community).
