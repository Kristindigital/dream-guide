## Part 1: Add-ons and upsells (only with a real second product)

An add-on lets a buyer who's already saying yes add one more thing that helps. Done honestly, it's a service, not a trick: the mom buying the budget planner may be glad to add the debt payoff tracker in one tap.

**Only offer an add-on if:**
- **It already exists.** Never sell a product that isn't made yet.
- **It solves the next problem for the same buyer** (`why-people-buy.md`, section 13). It fits what they just chose: the lunchbox planner with the dinner planner, not a budgeting sheet.
- **It's their own product,** or one they have the right to sell.

**No second product yet?** That's normal for a first product. Note the next-product idea in their Decisions and skip the add-on. Don't build one now unless they want to: one product, finished and selling, comes first.

### The four kinds (simplest first)

| Kind | What the buyer sees | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Bundle** | One product made of two or three that belong together, at a better price than buying each. | Every platform, including Etsy. |
| **Order bump** | A checkbox on the checkout page: "Yes, add the lunchbox planner for $4." One tap, same payment. | A small add-on, priced below the main product. |
| **One-click upsell** | A page right after paying, before the thank-you page: one bigger next step, with "Yes, add it" and "No thanks". No card details again. A **downsell** is a smaller option shown only after a "no". | A bigger next step: the full bundle, a course, a membership. |
| **Thank-you offer** | A mention of the next product on the thank-you page, in the delivery email, or on the "What's next" page inside the product. | Every platform. The gentlest kind. |

**Pricing, as a rule of thumb:**
- An order bump is a small yes, usually priced below the main product.
- An upsell can be bigger, but keep it a one-time price. Kristin's finding: one-time offers are an easier decision than monthly ones.
- A discount only if it's real ("$4 today instead of $6" only if it's $6 everywhere else).

### Honesty rules for every add-on

1. **The box is never ticked for them.** The buyer ticks it.
2. **The price is clear** before they tap, and the total shows before they pay. Some boxes (Systeme.io's, for one) only show the price in the total after ticking, so write it in the add-on's text: "Add it for $4."
3. **"No thanks" is easy and plain.** No guilt lines like "No thanks, I'll keep stressing about money".
4. **One add-on at a time,** never a chain of pop-ups.
5. **No fake timers, "only today" or "spots left"** on add-on pages.
6. **The add-on must be delivered as reliably as the main product,** in the delivery option chosen for it (Apply Round 8), to buyers who chose it, and only to them.

### Writing it

- **Order bump:**
  - **Headline:** first person, 4 to 10 words ("Yes! Add the lunchbox planner").
  - **One or two lines:** what it does for them.
  - **Price.**
- **Upsell page:**
  - **Headline:** the one benefit.
  - **One line:** why it fits what they just bought.
  - **What's inside:** 3 lines.
  - **Price.**
  - **Buttons:** "Yes, add it" and "No thanks".
  - Same honesty rules as the sales page (`sales-pages.md`).

### Systeme.io: building add-ons with the connector

Plan the whole funnel before creating any step. **Steps can't be reordered with the connector, and new steps always land at the end.**
- **With an upsell:** sales page → order form → upsell → (downsell) → thank-you page.
- **Already built without one?** Create the upsell step, then tell them to drag it into place in the funnel editor.

**Order bump:**
1. Create the add-on's price plan and digital product.
2. Update the order form's offer with the bump. One bump per offer.
3. Add the order bump block to the order form page, with the headline and lines written above, the box unticked.

**Upsell step:** it has its own offer, so it needs its own price plan and digital product. The page needs only the yes/no choice, never a full sales page.

**Delivering the add-on only to people who bought it:** the thank-you page is the same for everyone, so don't put the add-on's link there. Instead:
- give the add-on's digital product a **tag** as its resource
- create an automation rule: when that tag is added → send the add-on's delivery email

## Part 2: The free-to-paid path (Kristin's way, made simple)

**What it is:** a free resource (the freebie) → they give their name and email → they're shown the low-price product right away → thank-you page → later, the next step.
**Why it works:**
- People who've paid even a small amount are far more likely to buy again.
- The email list belongs to the student, not to a social app.
- If they ever run ads, small sales can cover part of the ad cost. That's a possibility, never a promise.

**When:** in Market, once their first product is for sale. Or in Apply, if they chose Systeme.io, Stan or Beacons for an email list and already have a freebie.
**Where it works:** Systeme.io, Stan Store, Beacons, Payhip and Gumroad. **Not Etsy:** Etsy doesn't allow collecting buyers' emails for marketing or sending them elsewhere. **Skool:** a free community already works as the freebie.

### The pieces

1. **The freebie** solves one small, quick piece of the same problem, and leads naturally to the paid product.
   - Example: "The 1-page payday checklist" (free), then the $7 budget planner.
   - Built in Canva in their look, one to five pages.
2. **The opt-in page:** short, with no big promise, no long copy and no video.
   - **Headline:** what they get, plus the result ("Free: the 1-page payday checklist").
   - **A picture of the freebie,** and a line on why they made it.
   - **Name and email,** then the button.
   - Proof only if it's real and about the freebie itself.
3. **The offer page, straight after they sign up:**
   - "Your free list is on its way to your inbox."
   - Then the low-price product, one time (under about $50; $7 to $27 is common), with one button.
   - Use `sales-pages.md`, kept short.
4. **The thank-you page:** confirm, then one next step.
5. **The first email delivers the freebie.**
   - **Never put the freebie's download on the page itself** (Kristin's rule). People would grab it and leave before seeing the offer.
   - When they open the email to get it, their inbox learns to trust the sender, so later emails are more likely to land in the main inbox.
   - A Canva public view link is a good way to deliver a free resource.
6. **A short welcome email series:** each email with one purpose and one link, every claim true.
   - People who already follow them (warm) need fewer emails: Kristin uses about 5 to 10.
   - Strangers from ads (cold) need more: about 20 to 30.
   - **For a beginner:** start with 3 to 5 and add more later.

**Optional, later:** a short quiz right after the opt-in, to show each person the product that fits their answer (with tags). It's powerful, but skip it until they have two or more products. Kristin's own storefront quiz is an example.

### The launch order (Kristin's findings)

This one belongs to Market: start warm, then go colder.
1. Tell a handful of friends, followers and past customers first. An early-bird price is fine if it's real.
2. Get the first sales, and help those buyers get the result.
3. Bring it to the rest of their warm audience.
4. Only then go to strangers (cold), and raise the price to the regular price (their "after the first 10 sales" price from Research).
